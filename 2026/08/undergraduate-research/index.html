My Undergraduate Research Journey and Reflections

Created2026-08-21|Updated2026-09-18|Experience
|Word Count:1.3k|Reading Time:8mins|Post Views:

As an undergraduate at Ocean University of China, I met my research advisor, Gao Feng, purely by chance in my sophomore year at a student club event. He is, I’d say, the person who set me on the path of academia.

I major in Computer Science and Technology. I started university in 2023, just as AI was entering a period of explosive growth, and I quickly became fascinated by this rapidly emerging field.

By nature, I’m not very good at striking up conversations, nor am I naturally very proactive. Professor Gao and I spoke from time to time, but not very often. Still, that invisible hand of fate seemed to keep pushing me forward. Then came another coincidence: Professor Gao became the instructor for our major’s Academic English: Research Paper Writing course (I only learned later that he had simply been roped into it 😂), which gave us more opportunities to get to know each other. Through our conversations, I learned that AI can roughly be divided into several major areas, including natural language processing (NLP), computer vision (CV), and somewhat less widely known fields such as advertising and recommendation systems.

Since I was also pursuing a second degree in Robotics alongside computer science, I’d gradually built up some experience in vision-related development. When I learned that this was precisely Professor Gao’s area of expertise, I wasted no time asking to learn from him — when you find a great mentor, you hold on to the opportunity, haha.

Professor Gao’s work applies computer vision to remote sensing. At first, I had no idea what remote sensing even was — I only knew it had something to do with geography (to be honest, I was pretty panicked). But then I thought: it was related to vision, after all. Besides, I was in a slightly awkward position at the time, with nothing particularly impressive to show, whether in terms of my GPA or extracurricular competitions. Getting involved in research would at least give me a competitive edge, so I decided to stick with it.

Another person I feel very lucky to have met was the graduate student Professor Gao introduced me to, whom I usually call Senior Gong. When I first arrived at university, I had planned to throw myself into algorithm competitions, so I joined the OUC CCF student chapter’s group chat. It turned out that the person who was always sharing events and articles there was Senior Gong. Now I was meeting him in person, and, thanks to Professor Gao’s introduction, he was helping me find my feet in research. I knew right away how lucky I was to have met someone like him. In my mind, Senior Gong was the leading figure in the research group — surely he wouldn’t otherwise be managing a university-wide group chat with several hundred members (Senior Gong, if you’re reading this, please don’t tag me ✋😭🤚).

Ahem. My path into research may have been rather comical, even haphazard, but back to the point: once I truly stepped through that door, I certainly wasn’t spared any of the hard work.

I began with online courses, which gave me a grounding in the fundamentals of deep learning. I then started attending the research group’s regular meetings, which helped me understand more quickly how the senior students continued to innovate in this field. Meanwhile, Professor Gao would periodically send me cutting-edge papers related to computer vision. Many focused on other areas, such as medicine, but I still found them highly inspiring. Over time, as my experience grew, Professor Gao and I eventually agreed on a target journal and the broad direction of the project.

Let me briefly explain why I chose to submit to IEEE GRSL (Geoscience and Remote Sensing Letters) rather than TGRS (Transactions on Geoscience & Remote Sensing). Judging by headline metrics such as impact factor, TGRS may appear more prestigious than GRSL, but people who work in remote sensing know that the two are “sister journals”: TGRS publishes longer papers that generally call for around three novel contributions, while GRSL has a five-page limit and two can be enough. I would certainly have liked to submit to TGRS — after all, it’s a CAS Q1 journal, while GRSL was Q2 (it dropped to Q3 in 2026 🫠, though I console myself that it’s still JCR Q1). Unfortunately, time wouldn’t allow it. I needed to get the paper out before the end of my junior year because I planned to go abroad during my senior year. By the time the plan was finalized, I had only eight months left to run the experiments and write the first draft, while TGRS’s review process alone takes at least half a year. There simply wasn’t enough time, so I had to settle on GRSL and its shorter, faster format. Honestly, for an undergraduate, publishing a CAS Q1/Q2 SCI paper is already a major accomplishment and an honor, so I didn’t have much reason to be disappointed. That was what I got for entering university with no plan whatsoever — a perfect demonstration of the saying “30 percent effort, 70 percent fate”, haha.

After running the experiments, sorting out the data, and finishing the first draft, Professor Gao helped me polish the paper further, and we successfully submitted it on <2026/05/21>. After a long and agonizing wait, we received a total of 16 revision requests from three reviewers on <2026/07/01>. After discussing them with Professor Gao, I designed additional validation experiments to address each point, revised the manuscript, and drafted the corresponding Response Letter. This happened to coincide with finals month at university. Thankfully, I didn’t have many exams, although studying for them still took some time away from the paper revision. It is worth noting that authors are given only three weeks to respond to reviewers’ comments, so every single minute mattered. Finally, with one week left, I submitted all the revised materials on <2026/07/15>.

Afterwards, summer break began and I went home, so my communication with Professor Gao moved online. After more than a month of waiting, the message finally arrived:

“I am pleased to inform you that

GRSL-01407-2026.R1 Semantic-Guided Fusion Network for Multi-source Remote Sensing Image Classification

has been accepted for publication in the IEEE Geoscience and Remote Sensing Letters. It will be published in the next available issue.”

I was absolutely thrilled that day — it finally felt as though all the hard work had paid off. That was <2026/08/19>. Less than a week later, I could find my own paper on IEEE Xplore. Mission accomplished, haha.

I’m writing this post for two main reasons. First, I want to preserve the memory of my undergraduate research journey so that, years or even decades from now, I can return to it and relive this part of my life. Second, I hope to encourage students who want to get involved in research as undergraduates. Once you enter university, I hope you’ll map out the next few years as early as possible and avoid going into important battles unprepared. There’s a saying I’m very fond of, roughly: “People often say that ordinary people get fewer opportunities, and that is true. What we can do is be ready to seize them when they arrive. The key is to keep putting ourselves in a position where the odds are in our favor. There may be setbacks along the way, but the better the odds, the more likely we are to make the most of an opportunity when it comes.”

My thanks to everyone who has helped me along the way 🌹
